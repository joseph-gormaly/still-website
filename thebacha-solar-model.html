The Model

Every diagram of the solar system lies about distance

To fit the planets onto one page, a poster compresses the distances until they look like neighbours on a street. Visitors arrive believing the solar system is crowded. It is almost entirely empty — and emptiness is the one fact a page or a screen cannot carry, because both are built to delete dead space.

Wood Buffalo already owned the right apparatus: a circular observing deck at Pine Lake, 9.51 m across, and 62 km of road to Fort Smith. Declaring that deck to be the Sun fixes the model's only free parameter. Every other number then follows by arithmetic — a ratio of 1 : 146,309,148, with nothing left to choose.

Make the deck the Sun, and the drive out to it stops being travel time. It becomes the model.

The result is uncomfortable, which is the point. All four rocky planets fall inside the first 1.6 km. Neptune, the outermost, sits at 30.75 km — barely halfway to town, leaving 32 km of highway that is not in the solar system at all. Each sign carried a sphere built to the same ratio, so the object in a visitor's hand and the deck kilometres away belonged to one model: Earth an 8.7 cm ball, Mercury 3.3 cm, Jupiter just under a metre.

Interactive

The model on the ground

Each planet sits at its true modelled position on the real road. Pan and zoom; the spheres keep their true proportions to one another at every magnification.

Spheres are magnified for visibility but share one multiplier, so every size relationship in the set is preserved — Jupiter really is 29 times Mercury's diameter at any slider position. The dashed ring around each planet is a fixed-size locator, not the planet: where a planet is only a few pixels across inside its ring, that is the honest answer. Saturn's rings are drawn to the same scale, which is why its silhouette outreaches Jupiter's even though its body is smaller. Route traced from OpenStreetMap road centrelines; distances measured along the drivable route from the deck.

Design Rationale

An exhibit you cannot scroll past

Science communication has largely accepted the terms of the attention economy: eight seconds, a hook, a fact that fits in a caption. That format selects for the compressible and filters out anything whose entire meaning is duration. Emptiness cannot be communicated quickly. That is what emptiness is.

This takes about forty-five minutes to cross and cannot be skimmed. Its most important stretch — the 21 km between Saturn and Neptune, where nothing whatsoever happens — is by any engagement metric a failure. It is also the whole lesson: the boredom is load-bearing. There is a symmetry in siting that argument in a dark sky preserve, since darkness and sustained attention are both scarce now, and for related reasons.

Built with the Thebacha and Wood Buffalo Astronomical Society for the 6th annual Dark Sky Festival, August 17–21, 2017. The partnership is why it has a social life — interpretive hardware no local group uses becomes roadside furniture within a season. It also leaves a worked example on the road to the swimming lake, which matters more than the model itself: every number here comes from one measurement and one ratio, arithmetic a middle-school student can carry out completely and then own, in a way that being shown a poster never achieves.

Notes & Caveats

Honest limits of the model

  • Distances are mean, and the line is not the orbit. Positions use semi-major axes, and laying the solar system along a road collapses a plane into a line: the signs encode distance from the Sun, not the planets' positions relative to one another.
  • Marker positions are reconstructed, not surveyed. The signs stood at the roadside; map positions are interpolated along the drivable route at each planet's modelled distance from the deck.
  • Pluto did not fit, and coordinates are approximate. At 39.48 AU it lands 40.37 km out, past Neptune, and is listed for reference only. Deck coordinates are good to about 100 m; the Fort Smith endpoint is an estimate of the office location.
